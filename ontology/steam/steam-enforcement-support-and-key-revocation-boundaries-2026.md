# Steam publication restrictions, support and key revocation: different remedies

**Reviewed October 28, 2026.** [Machine-readable policy register](steam-enforcement-support-and-key-revocation-boundaries-2026.json) · [verification tests](../../tests/steam-enforcement-support-boundaries.test.mjs). The official pages below document public process, not private partner contracts or a representative enforcement dataset.

## Failed store/build review is not account suspension

Valve's [review process documentation](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/review_process?l=english) requires review of the store page and product build before release. Its normal review guidance suggests allocating **at least seven business days** for each submission, including possible feedback and revisions. The page says that, after approval, subsequent routine updates do not require the same initial review. This cannot be taken as a promise of immunity from later enforcement.

The [Steamworks onboarding guidance](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/onboarding) separately restricts content for which the publisher lacks rights, illegal materials, malware, fraud and other disallowed product categories. Having signed onboarding documents or passed an initial technical build review cannot establish valid IP title, a final judicial conclusion, or guaranteed store permanence.

## Contacting Valve is documented; guaranteed appeals are not

[Valve's developer contact documentation](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/help/contact?l=english) points developers to Steamworks Support tickets and developer discussions. That is a public support route for unresolved publishing questions. **It is not proof** of a universal adjudicatory appeals process, a fixed deadline for reinstatement, a success rate or a contractual right to an independent decision.

A claim that a game was refused, a bank payout held, an account restricted or content removed requires case-specific grounds, documents and affected dates. These different events must not be pooled into one 'ban probability'; private partner agreements and financial holds are outside this public evidence.

## Retirement is not an involuntary takedown

In the [product-retirement guide](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/retire_app?l=english), Valve directs a developer who wants to stop selling a title or has lost distribution rights to contact its team, explain the circumstances, notify external Steam-key retailers and stop further retail-key sales. Existing customers normally **retain access in their Steam libraries** even when the game is removed from store listing and the buy button disappears. This does not guarantee future servers or functionality.

Valve may extend refunds when canceled games or promised content are not delivered. That potential customer redress must be treated as a separate uncertainty rather than a universal fixed refund rate or evidence that all retired games returned their entire gross.

## Revoking keys is constrained by rights and legitimate customers

The [Steam Keys guide](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/keys?l=english) distinguishes unused keys, keys issued to temporary beta testers, fraudulent/stolen keys and keys legitimately purchased by customers. It warns against revoking valid customer keys and restricts administrative operations to appropriately authorized key batches. This is about **license access**, not a measure of paid Steam-checkout refund volume.

**Unresolved empirical questions:** how many first releases are rejected, proportion subsequently accepted, involuntary distribution removals, actual publisher revenue holds, outcome and timing of disputes, and average loss from fraudulent keys. None are available as a representative dataset in the reviewed official public documents.
