# Steam indie labor: professional workweeks versus actual hours to ship a game

**Reviewed October 28, 2026.** [Structured population crosswalk](professional-hours-versus-shipping-labor-2026.json). This research adds **external survey evidence** about game-work schedules while refusing to present incompatible measures as a typical Steam game's production time.

## GDC 2026: workweek lengths, not per-game production totals

The [GDC 2026 State of the Game Industry report, page 47](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-01-29-dec052f4_d88e_48ce_9f83_a18ce2f2a6e5_541400_GDC26_PDF_SOTI_Report.pdf) surveyed **more than 2,300 game industry professionals** across job families. Under its revised weekly-hours questions, about **59% of non-solo professionals** worked at least 40 hours on games in an average week, and **11%** typically worked 50 or more. Separately, **35% of a mixed group of contractors, consultants and solo developers** reported average weeks under 30 hours. **37% of solo-developer respondents** reported *at least one* 60-plus-hour week in the previous year.

The denominators differ: a mixed solo/contractor subgroup is not the whole employed non-solo group; maximum week is not average week; **working 40 hours in a given week cannot tell us whether one complete commercial release required 300 or 3,000 total hours**. GDC specifically says its workweek categories and questions changed in 2026, preventing reliable year-over-year comparison with the 2025 survey.

## Unity 2026: active editor project hours are not shipped-game hours

[Unity's 2026 Game Development Report](https://unity.com/resources/gaming-report) combines two quite different sources: a **300-developer Cint survey** across team sizes and engine types, and proprietary Unity ecosystem data from **nearly five million developers active in 2025**.

The Unity report describes a reduction in its editor-activity project-hours median from about **91 hours in January 2022** to **21 hours in December 2025**. It also reports that **67% of surveyed developers** said they prototype for three months or less. Neither statistic measures the hours required to **create, sell, support and maintain one paid Steam game**. Editor activity can omit external programming tools, planning, asset production, outsourced labor, localization, QA, store artwork and publishing, user support, administration, and abandoned or idle projects. Prototyping is only one stage of game development.

This especially matters for indie opportunity-cost accounting: using **21 Unity editor hours** as a finished-game labor budget would be an unjustified shortcut.

## What a defensible real developer-hour denominator would require

For a fixed Steam first-paid-release cohort, the relevant labor unit is cumulative project-person hours across all contributors through a specified post-release horizon, plus cost treatment for unpaid founder hours, contractors, software, marketing and maintenance. An unfinished or abandoned game must be handled explicitly when estimating the economic prospects of game development as an occupation, rather than looking only at successful shipped games. A complete estimate also needs a verified legal publisher share and real refund-adjusted cash, not revenue-model outcomes.

The existing [synthetic first-game break-even scenarios](synthetic-first-game-break-even-scenarios-2026.md) deliberately assume a hypothetical number of hours and hourly wage as *sensitivity inputs*, not measured typical work. The new survey figures help illustrate **why a single median hour count cannot be asserted** from public workweek or telemetry reports alone.

**Still unknown:** representative hours to complete and commercially launch a first paid Steam game, opportunity-cost-adjusted developer income per hour, legal-entity profit and annual salary-equivalent returns. No respondent-level, employee, editor or publisher financial records were collected.
